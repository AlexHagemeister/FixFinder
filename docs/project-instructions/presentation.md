## Final Presentation

On **Tuesday, December 8**, in **1420 Gateway**, your team will present its final interface design in person to the teaching staff, your classmates, and invited guests. Come see the tremendous work of your classmates!

### In Your 8 Minutes, Make Sure You:

1.  **Introduce** your team and project.
2.  **Motivate** the need for your design, and describe your users and their context.
3.  **Show some of your process**, such as interviews, observations, sketches or mockups. Be selective: pick the one or two examples that best build your design case.
4.  **Tell us your three primary tasks.** State each one clearly: who the user is, what situation they're in, and what they're trying to get done.
5.  **Walk us through at least two of those tasks** in your working design, from start to finish. Use your in-context video for at least one of them. Show the other with video, a screen recording, or a live demo. Your design should look and feel final and high-fidelity.
6.  **Show how AI works in your design**, including what it does for your users and how the interface handles its output.
7.  **Present competing products or existing solutions**, and explain how yours is different.
8.  **Explain why we should care** about your design.
9.  **Finish strong!**

Plan on spending at least half your time on the task walkthroughs. Keep process and competitive analysis brief.

### Presenting From Your Own Laptop

Each team presents from its own laptop. The schedule is tight, so:

- **Set up during the critique of the team before you**, so you're ready to start the moment it's your turn. The schedule has no extra time for setup, and your 8 minutes start on time whether or not you're ready. Teams presenting in Slot 1 should arrive early and test their connection before the session starts.
- **Test your full setup ahead of time**: display mirroring, resolution, audio, and video playback. Don't rely on Wi-Fi if you can avoid it; download your video and play it locally. If you want presenter notes figure it out ahead of time not during the presentation time.
- **Close everything else.** Turn off notifications, quit chat and email apps, and close unrelated tabs before you connect.
- **Have one laptop ready as a backup**, ideally a second teammate's, with the same slides and video on it.

### What to Demo

Designers are often asked to envision a final design and communicate it to an audience: a professor, a design team, a manager, potential investors. Our primary critique will focus on your storytelling, technical skills, and UI design and how well you implemented it.

You don't need to handle every case and screen. This is not a product launch. But we do expect **working code for your three primary tasks**, implemented at high fidelity. Each task should work well enough to show a client or run a user test on it. Don't spend five days getting a complex background process to handle every edge case while the UI goes unpolished. If you're unsure what you need to implement, check with us.

If your design depends on data, enter enough realistic data to make the demo compelling. No "User1," "User2," variations of Eric Paulos, or Lorem Ipsum.

Your AI features must be real, working model calls, not mock-ups. Show how your interface labels AI-generated content and how it handles a slow or wrong response.

You may demo your design live, either on your laptop or with a phone mirrored to it. If you do, have a screen recording of the same task ready in case the live demo, the network, or the model fails.

### Presentation

- Use slides. Replace text with images wherever possible, and make sure any text can be read from the back of the room.
- Cover the required content within 8 minutes. Practice and time your presentation. You will be cut off at 8 minutes and lose points for going over.
- Face the audience, make eye contact, and speak loudly enough to be heard at the back of the room. Don't read from notes or your phone.
- Keep everything inside your presentation. Avoid jumping between windows, browser tabs, or apps, except for a planned live demo.

### Video

Include a short video (about 60–90 seconds) of your design in use, in context, showing at least one of your primary tasks. Record on a real phone, or whatever device your design targets, in the setting where it would actually be used. A screen recording alone doesn't show context. Keep the camera steady: use a tripod, or brace your hand against a surface.

- **Landscape video often works best**
- Either let the video play with its own narration, or talk over a silent video. Don't do both.
- Embed the video in your slides

### Grading Guidelines

**Introduction and delivery**

- Did the team introduce themselves and their project?
- Were handoffs between team members smooth?
- Was the presentation on time and well paced?
- Was the team set up and ready on time, and did they work with their laptop, slides, video, and demo without technical struggles?

**Users and motivation**

- Did the team choose an interesting context, the right target group, and a good application space?
- Did they clearly describe their user group and context of use?
- Did they motivate the need for their design? Why should anyone care?
- Did they describe the user research and studies they performed?
- How does the design differ from existing apps and solutions? Was a competitive analysis provided?

**Design and implementation**

- Did the team clearly state their three primary tasks, and walk through at least two in enough detail to evaluate the interface?
- Was a video shown of the design being used in an appropriate context for at least one task?
- Was the interface novel and creative, and appropriate for the tasks it supports?
- Did the team show enough of the final UI to evaluate it?
- Were the final UI and user experience consistent with the user needs they described?
- Was the UI implemented with a high level of detail and design (fonts, colors, icons, layout)?
- Did the AI features work, and did they fit the users' needs?

**Presentation design**

- Was the visual design of the slides clear, consistent, and professional? Were images, text, video, and icons well designed and polished? Were there spelling, grammar, or visual errors that distracted from the presentation?
- Did the presentation end well and leave us excited and wanting more?
